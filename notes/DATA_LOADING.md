Went and traced through the actual mechanics rather than guessing further — it's not the prefetcher, and it's not really a "bug" anywhere in torchure. It's a hard limitation in how HF datasets resumes a streamed JSONL shard, and there are three resume layers stacked on top of each other in our pipeline (shuffle → split → map/pack), and only one of them is actually expensive:

1. Raw shard layer (ExamplesIterable.__iter__, iterable_dataset.py:311-321) — this is the expensive one:
for gen_kwargs in islice(_split_gen_kwargs(...), shard_idx_start, None):
    shard_example_idx_start = self._state_dict["shard_example_idx"] ...
    for key_example in islice(self.generate_examples_fn(**gen_kwargs), shard_example_idx_start, None):
1. It does skip whole shards for free — shards before shard_idx_start are never touched. But within the current shard, islice(generator, shard_example_idx_start, None) has no shortcut: a JSONL file streamed over HTTP has no line index or byte offsets recorded anywhere, so the only way to reach record N is to actually request, download, and json.loads() records 0..N-1 first and throw them away. If a worker was thousands of records into its shard when it checkpointed, resuming means re-downloading and re-parsing that entire prefix. This is exactly the multi-GB, multi-minute cost you watched — there's no config flag or code fix for it, it's inherent to treating an HTTP text stream as resumable.
2. Shuffle buffer layer — cheap. It doesn't try to restore its actual buffered contents (that's the "will be refilled" warning), it just refills 1000 fresh items from the now-resumed raw layer above. Bounded by shuffle_buffer: 1000.
3. Map/pack layer (MappedExamplesIterable._iter, iterable_dataset.py:1583-1613) — also bounded, but with a wrinkle: it only checkpoints its own position at pack_batch boundaries (ours is 1000), and the skip-discard happens after apply_function() runs (line 1592, then the discard loop at 1607-1613). So on resume it also re-tokenizes and re-packs up to one full pack_batch (1000 raw docs) just to throw away outputs already seen — real recompute, but small (seconds, not minutes) compared to #1.

So the honest answer to "why can't we resume the dataloader": we can, and it does land exactly back where it was — but "resuming" a streamed JSONL shard fundamentally means paying to re-read everything already consumed in that shard, because there's no seek. The resume_dataloader: false flag we added isn't a monkey-patch around a bug — it's trading that unavoidable cost away by accepting a fresh shuffle position instead.
